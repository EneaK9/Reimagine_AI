# AI Yard Designer — Scenario Decision Guide

> For every input the user provides, this document defines what the AI must do differently. This is the logic engine behind the product selection, layout decisions, material choices, plant recommendations, and image generation rules. Read every relevant section before producing any output.

---

## How to Use This Document

Each section maps to one user input. Inside each section, every possible answer has a defined set of consequences — what changes in the product search, the design rules, the image generation, and the warnings the AI must show.

Inputs combine. A coastal + small space + renter + $500 budget + dogs produces a very specific set of constraints. Run every applicable section and merge the outputs before searching for a single product.

# MODULE 1 — SPACE INPUTS

---

## 1.1 — Space Dimensions

### Under 15 m² (micro yard, small balcony, tiny patio)

**What this means:** Every product must justify its footprint. There is no room for duplication or decorative-only items.

**Design rules:**
- Maximum 1 large piece of furniture (a bistro set OR a small sofa, never both)
- All planters must be vertical, wall-mounted, or railing-mounted — never floor planters that eat into circulation space
- No pergolas, gazebos, or overhead structures unless the space is entirely paved and the structure IS the ceiling
- Foldable or stackable furniture is strongly preferred — show folded state in image notes
- No lawns, no gravel paths — surface treatment is one material only
- Minimum clear circulation path of 90cm must be preserved after all products are placed

**Product search filters:**
- Filter for "balcony", "small space", "compact", "foldable", "stackable" in all furniture searches
- Maximum table diameter 70cm for round, 60×90cm for rectangular
- Prioritise wall-mounted planters, railing planters, vertical garden panels
- Lighting must be string lights, solar stake lights, or clip-on — no large floor lamps

**Image generation:**
- Shoot angle must be wide-angle from the doorway looking out — do not use a perspective that hides how small the space is
- Show the space with furniture in use (people-scale implied) so the user understands actual livability
- Negative space around furniture must be clearly visible — never pack the visualisation

**Warnings to show user:**
- "This space is under 15m². We've prioritised products that keep circulation clear and avoid overwhelming the area. Everything shown can be moved or removed easily."

---

### 15–40 m² (small garden, average urban backyard)

**What this means:** The most common residential garden size. Enough room for a defined seating area plus one or two additional zones.

**Design rules:**
- Maximum 2 defined zones (e.g. dining area + planting zone, or seating area + lawn)
- One statement piece allowed (fire pit, water feature, statement planter grouping, pergola over dining area)
- Lawn area should not exceed 50% of total space if other zones are included
- Paths between zones minimum 90cm wide
- Fence or boundary treatment on at least one wall (trellis, climbing plant support, wall-mounted planters)

**Product search filters:**
- Standard residential furniture sizing appropriate
- Look for modular options — sofa sets that can be reconfigured
- Include one set of boundary/privacy products in every recommendation

**Image generation:**
- Use a 3/4 overhead angle showing both zones simultaneously
- Include human scale reference (implied furniture use, not literal people unless requested)

---

### 40–100 m² (medium garden, suburban backyard)

**What this means:** Enough space for genuine landscape design with multiple zones, a lawn, and structural planting.

**Design rules:**
- Up to 3 defined zones (e.g. dining terrace + lawn + planting border)
- A dedicated lawn area is appropriate and expected — recommend lawn treatment if the existing grass is poor
- At least one vertical element required (pergola, trellis, tall specimen plant, privacy screen)
- Lighting design becomes important — zone the lighting across areas, not just one string of lights
- A focal point is mandatory at this scale — statement tree, water feature, sculpture, or fire pit

**Product search filters:**
- Full-size dining sets (6-seater) appropriate
- Separate lounge and dining furniture sets are viable
- Include raised beds, large planters, border edging in search
- Outdoor rugs appropriate for defining seating zones

**Image generation:**
- Use a perspective view from one corner showing the full depth and width
- Show all zones simultaneously with clear visual separation between them
- Include a sense of journey — path or lawn leading the eye to the focal point

---

### Over 100 m² (large garden, estate-scale)

**What this means:** Multiple distinct rooms. Retail products from Amazon/Walmart are likely insufficient for the full space — professional landscaping elements may be needed.

**Design rules:**
- Divide into minimum 3 rooms/zones — each with its own purpose, furniture set, and planting scheme
- Hard landscaping (paving, gravel, decking) should define zone boundaries, not just implied spacing
- Trees are now appropriate as structural elements — include at least one if none exist
- Irrigation system should be recommended if any planting is significant
- Lighting design must cover all zones — pathway lights, zone lighting, feature lighting are all distinct products

**Product search filters:**
- Commercial or semi-commercial grade furniture appropriate for high-use areas
- Search for "garden room dividers", "outdoor rugs large", "garden arches", "pergola kits" as structural elements
- Water features at this scale can be larger — include pond kits, wall fountains, freestanding water features

**Warnings to show user:**
- "At this scale, some elements (paving, large structures, lawn preparation) may require professional installation. We'll flag those clearly."

## 1.2 — Orientation (Compass Direction)

### North-facing (Northern Hemisphere)

**What this means:** Receives little to no direct sun for most of the year. The coldest, most challenging orientation.

**Plant rules:**
- Only recommend shade-tolerant species: ferns, hostas, astilbe, hellebores, hydrangeas (certain varieties), ivy, fatsia japonica, bleeding heart
- No Mediterranean plants (lavender, rosemary, cistus, agapanthus) — they require full sun and will fail
- No vegetable growing unless there is a sunny corner — state this clearly
- Prioritise plants with interesting foliage over flowers — flowers need sun to perform

**Material rules:**
- Recommend lighter-coloured furniture and surfaces to reflect available light and prevent the space feeling gloomy
- Avoid dark timber decking — it will look permanently damp and cold
- Light-toned gravel, pale paving, or light wood decking recommended

**Structural rules:**
- No pergola over main seating area — it will make an already shaded space feel like a cave
- Glass or perspex screening preferred over solid fencing additions — keeps whatever light exists
- Mirror panels on walls (outdoor-rated) can dramatically increase perceived brightness

**Image generation:**
- Use a bright overcast light setting — not harsh midday sun (which this space never gets) and not dramatic dusk
- Keep the colour palette of products light and airy to compensate for the shade

**Warnings to show user:**
- "Your garden faces north, which means limited direct sunlight. We've selected shade-tolerant plants and lighter-toned products to keep the space feeling bright and welcoming."

---

### South-facing (Northern Hemisphere)

**What this means:** Maximum sun throughout the day. The most desirable orientation in temperate climates. Can become extremely hot in warm climates.

**Plant rules:**
- The widest range of plants is viable — Mediterranean, cottage garden, prairie, tropical all work
- In hot climates (Zone 8+): include drought-tolerant species (lavender, sedum, salvia, ornamental grasses) — high water-demand plants will struggle without irrigation
- Include at least one plant that provides shade or screening if the space is large — the user will want relief from full sun in summer

**Material rules:**
- UV resistance ratings are critical — check that all cushion fabrics, plastic components, and composite materials carry UV stability certification
- Dark metal furniture will become dangerously hot to touch in summer — recommend light-coloured metal or wood/composite
- Consider shade sail or pergola with canopy in warm climates — include as a recommended product

**Structural rules:**
- Shade structure (pergola, sail shade, parasol) should be recommended as a primary product, not an afterthought
- Position seating to have access to both sun and shade — do not place dining area in full unrelieved sun

**Image generation:**
- Use warm afternoon sunlight with clear shadows showing depth
- Show the shade structure in use if one is included

---

### East-facing

**What this means:** Morning sun, afternoon shade. Ideal for breakfast spaces. Hot summer afternoons are naturally shaded.

**Plant rules:**
- Suitable for most cottage garden plants, roses (which prefer morning sun to dry dew), and many shrubs
- Avoid very sun-hungry plants that need all-day sun — agapanthus, bougainvillea, most vegetables
- Morning dew stays longer — good for moisture-loving plants, but check for fungal disease risk on susceptible species

**Design rules:**
- Position primary seating to capture morning sun — the best hours in this orientation
- This is the ideal orientation for a breakfast terrace or morning coffee area — lean into it
- Afternoon can be cool and shaded — include a covered area if the user uses the space in the evening

**Image generation:**
- Use a morning light setting — low golden sun from the east, long shadows pointing west

---

### West-facing

**What this means:** Afternoon and evening sun. The best orientation for evening entertaining. Can be hot in summer afternoons.

**Plant rules:**
- Similar to south-facing in terms of range, but plants must tolerate the intense afternoon and evening sun
- Excellent for evening-use plants: night-scented stock, jasmine, nicotiana — include these as recommendations
- Avoid plants that wilt in afternoon heat without consistent watering

**Design rules:**
- This is the prime orientation for outdoor dining and evening entertaining — design around it
- Lighting is secondary to sun management — the evening sun provides natural atmosphere
- Shade sail or large parasol recommended for the 2–5pm window in summer

**Image generation:**
- Use a warm evening/golden hour light setting — this is what the user will experience most
- Show lighting products switched on even in the evening image, as this is when they'll be used

## 1.3 — Surface Type

### Grass / Lawn

**What this means:** Freestanding products only unless the user is willing to remove or cut into turf. Furniture may sink into soft ground.

**Product rules:**
- Recommend furniture feet pads or ground protectors for any metal or heavy furniture
- No ground-anchored pergola without confirming user is willing to dig into lawn
- Raised beds need a membrane layer underneath to prevent grass growing up through them
- Recommend lawn-edge products (steel edging, plastic border) to define zones cleanly
- If lawn is poor quality: include lawn repair kit, overseeding product, or artificial grass as an option depending on budget

**Installation notes to show user:**
- "Furniture placed directly on grass may sink after rain. We recommend furniture foot pads or a ground-level paving slab under each leg."

---

### Concrete / Paving (existing)

**What this means:** The most flexible surface for product placement. Anchor points may be available. No ground preparation needed.

**Product rules:**
- Adhesive or bolt-down anchor systems are available for pergolas and screens
- Outdoor rugs are strongly recommended to define zones and add warmth
- Drainage must be respected — never recommend products that block existing drain covers
- Check the paving condition: cracked, stained, or mismatched paving may need treatment — include paving paint, patio cleaner, or crack filler as budget allows

---

### Bare Soil / Mud

**What this means:** Ground preparation is required before any product can be properly placed. The AI must not skip this step.

**Mandatory first recommendations:**
- Ground cover solution: gravel, bark chip, paving slabs, or decking tiles — depending on budget
- Weed membrane under any loose material
- Note: No furniture should be placed directly on bare soil — it will sink, become muddy, and be unusable in wet weather

**Design rule:** Always include a surface treatment product as Priority 1 in the product list, before any furniture or planting recommendation.

---

### Gravel (existing)

**What this means:** Good drainage, clean base, minimal work needed.

**Product rules:**
- Use ground plates or wide feet under furniture to prevent sinking
- Recommend edging to contain gravel if not already present
- Raised beds and planters work well on gravel — include freely
- Adding more gravel or changing colour/grade is low cost and high impact — include if budget allows

---

### Decking (existing)

**What this means:** Most versatile finished surface. Check condition before recommending anything heavy.

**Product rules:**
- Recommend decking oil or stain if the boards look weathered — this is a cheap high-impact improvement
- Weight limits may apply on raised decks — flag if recommending large water features, heavy stone planters, or multiple heavy furniture items
- Non-slip strips recommended if there are steps or slopes
- No ground-anchored products without checking what is beneath the deck

---

### Mixed surfaces

**What this means:** Treat each zone separately using the rules above. Do not apply a single surface rule to the whole space.

---

## 1.4 — Slope / Level

### Flat

No special considerations. Apply all other rules normally.

---

### Gentle slope (under 10°)

**Product rules:**
- Furniture with adjustable feet preferred — or include adjustable foot pads as an accessory recommendation
- No large water features — they will tilt and leak
- Planting on the slope should include ground cover plants to prevent soil erosion (creeping thyme, sedum, vinca)
- Raised beds are excellent for sloped gardens — they create level planting surfaces

---

### Significant slope (over 10°)

**What this means:** Levelling or terracing is required before most products can be used safely. This is a structural issue, not just a product issue.

**Rules:**
- Recommend terracing solution as Priority 1: railway sleepers, retaining wall blocks, or gabion baskets
- Any seating area must be on a levelled platform — do not recommend furniture on a significant slope
- Steps or a path with steps must be included in the design if there is a level change
- Flag to the user: significant slopes may require professional groundworks before retail products can be installed

**Warnings to show user:**
- "Your garden has a significant slope. Before placing furniture or plants, a level platform or terracing solution is needed. We've included that as the first step in the product list."

# MODULE 2 — LOCATION AND CLIMATE INPUTS

---

## 2.0 — The Location Intelligence System

A city name alone is not a climate profile. "New York" is not the same as "Los Angeles" is not the same as "Miami" — and the differences between them change almost every decision the AI makes. This module defines the seven data layers the AI must retrieve for any city before selecting a single product or plant.

**The rule:** Never store just a city name. Query all 7 layers and store the values. These numbers drive every material, plant, product, and structural decision downstream.

---

### The 7 Data Layers — What to Search and Why

---

#### Layer 1 — USDA Hardiness Zone

**What to search:** `"USDA hardiness zone [city]"`

**What it returns:** A zone number from 3a (extreme cold) to 12b (tropical). Every plant sold in the US and most internationally carries a zone rating. If the zone doesn't match, the plant dies in winter.

**What it decides:**
- Which plants survive the winter outdoors
- Whether Mediterranean plants (lavender, rosemary, agapanthus) are viable
- Whether tropical plants (bougainvillea, bird of paradise, citrus) are viable
- Whether frost-proof ratings are required on ceramic and concrete planters

**Zone reference:**
| Zone | Minimum winter temp | Example cities |
|---|---|---|
| 3–4 | -40°C to -29°C | Minneapolis, Anchorage, Calgary |
| 5 | -29°C to -23°C | Chicago, Denver, Boston |
| 6 | -23°C to -18°C | St Louis, Pittsburgh, Philadelphia |
| 7 | -18°C to -12°C | New York City, Seattle, London |
| 8 | -12°C to -7°C | Portland OR, Seattle, Pacific Northwest |
| 9 | -7°C to -1°C | Los Angeles, Phoenix, Dallas |
| 10–11 | -1°C to +10°C | Miami, Honolulu, Puerto Rico |

---

#### Layer 2 — Annual Rainfall Pattern (total and seasonal distribution)

**What to search:** `"average monthly rainfall [city]"` — look at each month individually, not just the annual total.

**What it returns:** Monthly rainfall in mm, revealing whether rain is spread evenly or concentrated in a wet season with a defined dry period.

**What it decides:**
- Whether irrigation is mandatory, helpful, or unnecessary
- Whether raised beds need drainage layers (wet climates) or moisture-retention layers (dry climates)
- Whether self-watering planters are worth the cost premium
- Which plants survive without supplemental watering

**The critical distinction:** Total annual rainfall is misleading.
- Seattle: ~940mm/yr but July average is only 18mm — summer is functionally a drought. Irrigation needed May–September despite the "wet" reputation.
- New York: ~1,270mm/yr spread evenly across all months — irrigation helpful but rarely critical.
- Los Angeles: ~380mm/yr almost entirely October–March — a 5-month summer drought. Irrigation is not optional for any planting.

**Threshold rule:** Any summer month averaging under 30mm in a Zone 7+ location = recommend irrigation products.

---

#### Layer 3 — Summer High Temperature and Heat Index

**What to search:** `"average July high temperature [city]"` and `"heat index [city] summer"` for humid locations.

**What it returns:** Average maximum temperature in the hottest month, and perceived temperature accounting for humidity.

**What it decides:**
- Whether a shade structure is a luxury or mandatory infrastructure
- Whether dark metal furniture becomes dangerously hot to touch
- Whether cool-season vegetables (lettuce, spinach, peas) can survive summer or will bolt and fail
- Whether misting systems are appropriate to recommend
- Lawn species: cool-season fescue vs warm-season Bermuda/Zoysia

**Threshold rules:**
- Above 32°C (90°F) average high: shade structure is Priority 1 in the design
- Above 35°C (95°F): dark metal furniture is unsafe; light colours or wood/composite only
- Above 38°C (100°F): design entirely around shade; midday use is impractical without it
- High heat + high humidity (Houston, Miami): misting fans add moisture to already-saturated air — do not recommend
- High heat + low humidity (Phoenix, Las Vegas): misting fans are excellent and worth recommending

---

#### Layer 4 — Frost Dates (First and Last)

**What to search:** `"last spring frost date [city]"` and `"first autumn frost date [city]"`

**What it returns:** The average calendar dates of the last frost in spring and the first frost in autumn. Subtracting these gives the growing season in days.

**What it decides:**
- Whether annuals are worth planting (under 100 days = marginal; 140+ days = yes)
- When to start seeds indoors vs direct sow
- Whether frost-protection products (fleece, cloches, cold frames) are worth recommending
- Whether all furniture and cushions must be stored seasonally

**Growing season reference:**
| City | Last frost | First frost | Growing season |
|---|---|---|---|
| Minneapolis | May 15 | Oct 1 | 139 days |
| Chicago | May 5 | Oct 15 | 163 days |
| New York | Apr 5 | Nov 10 | 219 days |
| Los Angeles | Jan 21 | Dec 15 | 328 days |
| Miami | None | None | 365 days |

**Special case — microclimates within a city:** Urban areas are typically 2–4°C warmer than surrounding suburbs due to the urban heat island effect. A Manhattan rooftop garden is effectively one zone warmer than the New York zone rating suggests.

---

#### Layer 5 — Annual Sunshine Hours and UV Index

**What to search:** `"annual sunshine hours [city]"` and `"average UV index [city] summer"`

**What it returns:** Total hours of sun per year, and the peak UV index in summer months.

**What it decides:**
- Whether solar lighting products will actually work reliably
- What UV resistance rating is required on fabrics, plastics, and composite materials
- Whether sun-loving plants (Mediterranean, prairie) will perform well
- Whether sun-hungry food crops are viable in north-facing or overcast-prone cities

**Threshold rules:**
- Under 2,000 hrs/yr: solar lighting unreliable in winter months — recommend battery or mains alternatives
- 2,000–2,800 hrs/yr: solar lighting workable in summer, unreliable Oct–Mar
- Above 2,800 hrs/yr: solar lighting appropriate year-round
- UV index above 6 in summer: UV-rated materials strongly recommended
- UV index above 8: UV-rated materials mandatory; this applies to all fabrics, plastics, and composites

**Altitude multiplier:** UV intensity increases ~4% per 300m elevation. Denver at 1,600m has UV 21% higher than a sea-level city at the same latitude. Factor this in for any mountain or high-elevation city.

---

#### Layer 6 — Humidity Level (Average Relative Humidity in Summer)

**What to search:** `"average relative humidity [city] summer months"`

**What it returns:** Average relative humidity percentage during June–August (or the local warm season equivalent).

**What it decides:**
- Whether mould and mildew resistant fabrics are mandatory or recommended
- Whether untreated metal hardware will rust rapidly
- Whether misting fans are effective or counterproductive
- Whether wood products require more aggressive treatment schedules

**Threshold rules:**
- Below 50% RH: low humidity risk; standard outdoor materials acceptable; misting fans excellent
- 50–65% RH: moderate risk; solution-dyed acrylic fabrics recommended; annual metal treatment advised
- Above 65% RH: mould-resistant materials mandatory for all fabric products; marine-grade hardware required for coastal high-humidity cities
- Above 75% RH: treat as tropical conditions; same material standards as Zone 11+

**High-humidity US cities to flag automatically:** Miami (74–80%), Houston (70–75%), New Orleans (72–78%), Jacksonville (72%), Tampa (70%), Charleston (70%), Savannah (71%), Washington DC (63%).

---

#### Layer 7 — Prevailing Wind Speed and Direction

**What to search:** `"average wind speed [city]"` and `"prevailing wind direction [city]"`

**What it returns:** Average wind speed in km/h (or mph), and the direction from which wind most commonly arrives.

**What it decides:**
- Parasol base weight requirement
- Whether freestanding screens need heavy-duty weighted bases
- Whether lightweight planters need to be ruled out or weighted
- Whether a windbreak plant layer should be the first recommendation before any ornamental planting
- Prevailing wind direction tells the AI which side of the garden needs shelter first

**Threshold rules:**
- Under 12km/h average: standard products appropriate; standard parasol bases (15kg) acceptable
- 12–20km/h: heavy parasol bases required (25kg minimum); windbreak planting recommended for exposed spaces; freestanding screen stability check required
- Above 20km/h: wind-rated products only; no lightweight accessories; any freestanding structure needs ground anchoring or very heavy ballast
- Coastal cities: regardless of average wind speed, gusts can be significantly higher — always apply coastal wind rules at the coast

**Highest wind cities to flag automatically:** Chicago (17km/h avg), Boston (18km/h), Oklahoma City (17km/h), Amarillo (22km/h — windiest large US city), Honolulu (18km/h), Kansas City (15km/h).

|---|---|---|
| Zone | 6a | Cold winters but milder than same-latitude Midwestern cities due to Chinook winds |
| Rainfall | ~360mm/yr with spring peak | Irrigation needed May–September |
| Summer dry months | Jun–Aug moderately dry | Irrigation recommended for planted areas |
| July avg high | 31°C / 88°F with low humidity | Shade recommended but not mandatory |
| Frost dates | Last May 7 / First Oct 7 | 153-day season; shorter than Zone 6 average due to altitude |
| Sunshine hours | ~3,110/yr | Solar lighting excellent — among top US cities for solar viability |
| UV index (summer) | 9–10 (elevated due to altitude at 1,600m) | UV-rated materials mandatory — UV is 21% more intense at Denver's elevation than sea-level cities at the same latitude |
| Summer humidity | ~40% RH | Low risk; misting fans effective; mould risk low |

### Decision Rules by Layer Combination

Some combinations of layers compound their effect and require special handling:

**High heat + high humidity (Miami, Houston, Jacksonville):**
- Misting fans not appropriate — air is already saturated
- Extra emphasis on shade AND air circulation (pergola fans, strategic open screening)
- Evening-only use orientation
- Tropical material standards even if zone number is below 11

**High heat + low humidity (Phoenix, Las Vegas, Albuquerque):**
- Misting fans are a strong recommendation
- Evaporative cooling elements worth mentioning
- Drip irrigation for all planting without exception
- Xeriscape plant palette mandatory

**Short growing season + high UV altitude (Denver, Salt Lake City, Albuquerque):**
- UV protection stronger than zone number suggests
- Fast-establishing plants preferred — less time to establish before first frost
- Container growing allows plants to be brought in, extending the usable season

**High rainfall + cold winters (Chicago, Boston, Minneapolis):**
- Frost-rated drainage products required — water that pools and freezes will crack paving
- Waterproof cushion storage is mandatory, not optional
- Spring cleanup product (moss treatment, patio cleaner) worth including

**Coastal + any zone:**
- Marine-grade hardware overrides all other material decisions
- Salt air corrosion can destroy standard hardware within one season regardless of zone or humidity levels
- Wind rules apply at the coast regardless of city average wind speed

## 2.1 — Environment Type

### Coastal / Beachside

**What this means:** Salt air, sand, high winds, high UV, and frequent moisture. The most corrosive outdoor environment for products.

**Material rules — mandatory:**
- Marine-grade stainless steel (316 grade) only for any metal hardware — standard stainless (304) will pit and rust within months
- Powder-coated aluminium is excellent — lightweight, corrosion-resistant, and UV stable
- Teak and ipe are the only woods recommended without aggressive maintenance schedules
- No wrought iron, cast iron, or mild steel — they will rust visibly within one season
- All fabrics must be solution-dyed acrylic (Sunbrella or equivalent) — standard polyester will fade and degrade rapidly
- Rope products must be polyester, not natural fibre — natural rope rots in humid salt air

**Plant rules:**
- Choose salt-tolerant species: sea holly (eryngium), sea lavender (limonium), ornamental grasses (especially Ammophila and Carex), agapanthus, escallonia, tamarisk, pittosporum
- Avoid large-leaved plants in exposed positions — salt wind causes leaf scorch
- Windbreaks and shelter planting before exposed ornamental planting — establish protection first

**Design rules:**
- Wind is a product killer — any lightweight furniture, planters, or accessories must be weighted or secured
- Recommend furniture weights or securing straps as an accessory product
- Parasols in coastal environments must have a heavy base (minimum 25kg) and a wind-vent design

**Warnings to show user:**
- "Coastal environments are the most challenging for outdoor products. We've filtered exclusively for salt-air and corrosion-resistant materials. Standard outdoor furniture will deteriorate much faster at the coast."

---

### Suburban

**What this means:** The most common environment. Standard conditions apply. Privacy from neighbours is typically a consideration.

**Design rules:**
- Include privacy screening as a consideration — trellis, tall planters, bamboo screening, or fence toppers
- Noise from neighbours may be relevant — dense planting provides some acoustic buffering (though not much)
- HOA restrictions are most likely in suburban environments — flag this to the user

---

### Urban / City

**What this means:** Typically small spaces, hard surfaces, surrounding buildings, limited soil, possible air quality issues.

**Design rules:**
- Container gardening is likely the primary planting method — recommend high-quality potting mix and larger containers (plants in small pots dry out too quickly in urban heat islands)
- Urban heat island effect: cities are significantly warmer than surrounding areas — adjust plant recommendations accordingly (more drought-tolerant, heat-tolerant plants)
- Light pollution means ambient evening light is significant — lighting products need to be bright to have any effect
- Security may be a consideration — heavy, lockable, or permanently installed products preferred over lightweight portable items

---

### Rural / Countryside

**What this means:** Typically larger spaces, natural setting, possible wildlife access, no urban heat island.

**Design rules:**
- Wildlife may interact with the garden — rabbits, deer, foxes, or birds depending on region
- If deer are likely: avoid hostas, tulips, roses, and most new growth plants — they are primary deer food. Recommend deer-resistant species
- If rabbits are likely: include rabbit-proof raised bed kits or wire mesh liners as a product recommendation
- Blend with the surrounding landscape — highly formal or urban-style designs can look jarring in a rural context
- Natural materials (timber, stone, corten steel) tend to look most appropriate

---

### Mountain

**What this means:** Short growing season, intense UV at altitude, temperature swings between day and night, likely windy.

**Material rules:**
- All materials must handle frost — same rules as cold climate zones
- UV at altitude is significantly more intense than at sea level — all fabrics need high UV ratings
- Wind exposure is likely — same weighting and securing rules as coastal

**Plant rules:**
- Short growing season — choose fast-establishing, season-extending plants
- Alpine plants are naturally suited: sedums, sempervivums, aubrieta, rock cress, alpine phlox
- Container growing allows plants to be brought inside during late frosts

# MODULE 3 — USER AND LIFESTYLE INPUTS

## 3.1 — Who Uses the Space

### Children (under 12)

**Safety rules — mandatory:**
- No sharp metal edges on any furniture — look for rounded edge specifications
- No small decorative items (pebbles, small stones, gravel in accessible areas) that are choking hazards for under 3s
- No toxic plants — mandatory check for every plant recommended. Common toxic plants to exclude: foxglove, oleander, angel's trumpet, euphorbia, yew, laburnum, lily of the valley, aconitum
- No water features without a safety cover, cage, or grid unless the feature is a wall-mounted cascade with no standing water
- Fire pits must include a safety guard/mesh cover and must be placed minimum 2m from any play area
- No glass or ceramic items at ground level — all outdoor accessories should be plastic, metal, or stone

**Design rules:**
- Include a defined play zone if budget allows — artificial grass patch, bark chip softfall area, sand section
- Keep a clear sightline from the main seating area to any play zone — parents need to see children at all times
- Raised beds and planters should be either low enough for children to interact with (learning garden) or tall enough to be out of reach — mid-height beds at child eye level invite destruction
- Label any plants included with child-safety status in the product output

---

### Dogs

**What this means:** Digging, chewing, running, and toilet use will all happen.

**Design rules:**
- Gravel is not recommended as a primary surface — dogs track it indoors, it gets into paws, and some dogs eat it
- Artificial grass is the best low-maintenance surface for dogs — include drainage layer in specification
- Real lawn: recommend a tough grass species (rye grass, Bermuda) — fine ornamental grasses will be destroyed
- All plants at lower levels must be non-toxic to dogs — check every recommended plant against ASPCA toxic plant list
- Raised beds protect plants from dogs — include as a standard recommendation when dogs are mentioned
- Avoid lightweight planters — large dogs will knock them over
- Secure any compost or food-waste element — dogs will dig into it

**Common plants toxic to dogs to exclude:** daffodil bulbs, tulip bulbs, sago palm, oleander, azalea/rhododendron, grapes/raisins (fruit garden), macadamia (nut garden), onion/garlic family, rhubarb leaves

---

### Cats

**Design rules:**
- Cats will use raised beds as a toilet unless protected — include cat-deterrent matting or spiky mats as an accessory
- Cats enjoy vertical elements — include climbing structures, tall plants, and perching opportunities if the user wants to design for cats
- Cats are attracted to fine, loose soil — use bark chip mulch (they dislike the texture) instead of bare soil

**Plant notes:**
- Catnip and valerian will attract cats — only recommend these if the user explicitly wants to attract their own cat to a specific area
- Common plants toxic to cats: lilies (highly toxic — all species), yew, foxglove, oleander, azalea, cyclamen

---

### Elderly users or limited mobility

**Design rules — mandatory:**
- All paths minimum 90cm wide, 120cm preferred for wheelchair access
- Raised beds (60–80cm height) recommended — eliminates need to kneel or bend
- Non-slip surfaces mandatory on all paving and steps — include non-slip treatment product
- Steps must have a handrail recommendation if more than 2 risers
- Seating must have arm rests — deep lounge furniture without arms is very difficult to get out of
- Tables should be at standard height (72–75cm) — low coffee tables are impractical as primary dining
- Lighting of paths and steps mandatory if evening use is likely — trips and falls in outdoor spaces are a serious risk

**Product search filters:**
- Search for "raised garden bed" with height specifications
- "Non-slip patio treatment" as a mandatory accessory
- Furniture with arm rests and firm seating (not low slung)

---

### Frequent entertaining / guests

**Design rules:**
- Prioritise dining — a table that seats the realistic guest number is Priority 1
- Include a minimum of 2 more seats than the stated regular use number (people entertain more than they plan for)
- Lighting is a primary product, not an accessory — evening entertaining requires proper lighting across the whole space
- Include a drinks/side table or outdoor bar cart — no dining space works well without a surface for food staging
- A covered area (pergola with canopy, large parasol) extends entertaining viability across weather conditions significantly

---

### Solo relaxation / single adult

**Design rules:**
- A single high-quality lounger or armchair beats a full sofa set for this use case
- Prioritise comfort and sensory experience: sound (water feature), scent (fragrant plants), tactile (soft planting, natural materials)
- Lower budget needed for furniture — redirect spend toward planting, lighting, and sensory elements
- Privacy becomes more important — a secluded feel is desirable

---

## 3.2 — Primary Purpose

### Dining

**Product priority order:**
1. Dining table (sized correctly for user's typical group)
2. Dining chairs (one per regular diner + 2 extras)
3. Shade element (parasol or pergola with canopy)
4. Outdoor lighting over dining area
5. Side/serving table or outdoor kitchen element

**Sizing rules:**
- Allow minimum 60cm per person at a dining table
- Allow 90cm clearance behind each chair when pushed out for a person to walk past
- Table placement: minimum 1.2m from any wall or fence to allow chair pullout plus circulation

---

### Relaxing / lounging

**Product priority order:**
1. Outdoor lounge furniture (sofa, armchair, or lounger depending on space)
2. Side table (every seat needs a surface within reach)
3. Shade element
4. Outdoor rug to define the zone
5. Lighting (ambient, not task)

**Rules:**
- Deep seating (seat depth 60cm+) is appropriate for lounging — dining-height chairs are not comfortable for long relaxation sessions
- Include weather-resistant cushion storage recommendation
- Hammock or hanging chair is a high-impact, space-efficient option for small relaxation spaces — include if dimensions allow

---

### Children's play

**Product priority order:**
1. Safe, soft surface (bark chip, artificial grass, rubber tiles)
2. Play equipment sized to age group and space
3. Shade over play area (children overheat)
4. Seating for supervising adults adjacent to play area
5. Storage for outdoor toys

**Rules:**
- All play equipment must carry the relevant safety certification for the user's country (EN1176 in Europe, ASTM F1487 in USA)
- Check maximum fall heights against surface softness — rubber tiles or bark chip minimum under any climbing equipment
- Sun protection over the play area is not optional in Zone 8+ climates

---

### Growing food / kitchen garden

**Product priority order:**
1. Raised beds (sized to the space and the user's ambition)
2. Quality peat-free compost and top soil to fill beds
3. Irrigation (drip system or soaker hose)
4. Seed/plant recommendations by season and zone
5. Tool storage

**Rules:**
- Food growing requires minimum 6 hours of direct sun per day — if the space doesn't have this, state it clearly and suggest what can be grown in shade (salad leaves, herbs, some brassicas)
- Raised beds must be minimum 30cm deep for most vegetables, 45cm for root vegetables
- Never recommend food growing in an east or north-facing space without qualifying the limitations — most food crops will simply not produce

---

### Purely aesthetic / show garden

**Design rules:**
- Every product must justify its presence visually — function is secondary
- Focus spend on high-quality planters, statement plants, sculptural elements, and considered lighting
- A single dramatic plant combination in a quality planter is worth more than ten mediocre plantings across cheap pots
- Include an art or sculptural element if budget allows — even a simple large smooth boulder has more design impact than three cheap garden ornaments

## 3.3 — Maintenance Tolerance

### Under 30 minutes per week (low maintenance)

**Plant rules — mandatory:**
- Only low-maintenance plants: ornamental grasses, sedums, lavender (in appropriate climate), established shrubs, evergreen ground cover, ferns (shade)
- No annual bedding — it requires replanting every season
- No rose varieties that require regular deadheading and spraying
- No high-water-demand plants without irrigation
- Weed membrane under all mulched areas — mandatory

**Product rules:**
- Artificial grass is a valid and recommended recommendation — flag it as the lowest-maintenance lawn option
- Self-watering planters strongly recommended — include in every planting recommendation
- Composite or powder-coated aluminium furniture — never teak (requires annual oiling) or natural materials (require treatment)

**Warnings to show user:**
- "We've selected everything for under 30 minutes of maintenance per week. This means some plant choices and materials are specifically low-care — we've avoided anything that requires regular pruning, treating, watering, or seasonal replacement."

---

### 1–2 hours per week (medium maintenance)

**What this means:** The average engaged homeowner. A full mixed planting scheme is appropriate. Some seasonal tasks (mulching, pruning) are acceptable.

**Rules:**
- Mixed perennial and shrub planting appropriate
- Real lawn appropriate — include a basic lawn care schedule note
- Timber furniture appropriate — include care product recommendation (teak oil or hardwood decking oil)
- Include a seasonal task note with the design: "Prune X in spring, mulch beds in April, bring cushions in before first frost"

---

### Enjoys gardening (high maintenance)

**What this means:** Maintenance is not a constraint — it's part of the enjoyment.

**Rules:**
- Full planting palette available — include seasonal bedding, cut flower borders, fruit and vegetable growing
- Higher plant count and complexity appropriate — layered borders with bulbs, perennials, shrubs, and climbers
- Recommend composting system if space allows
- Include a tool recommendation if the user has mentioned they enjoy gardening

---

## 3.4 — Ownership Status

### Homeowner

No special restrictions. All products including permanent structures, ground anchors, wall fixings, and in-ground planting are appropriate subject to local rules.

**Additional check:**
- Flag HOA restrictions if suburban environment — ask the user to confirm before recommending any structure over 1.5m or any boundary modification

---

### Renter

**Rules — mandatory:**
- No products that require drilling, bolting, or fixing to walls, fences, or the ground
- No pergolas or structures that require concrete footings
- No permanent in-ground planting — all plants in containers only
- No alteration to existing paving, decking, or surfaces
- All furniture must be fully freestanding and movable
- Lightweight options preferred — the user may need to take everything when they leave

**Product search filters:**
- Search specifically for "freestanding", "no-drill", "portable", "container garden"
- Pergola alternative: freestanding outdoor canopy or gazebo with weighted bases — no ground fixing required
- Privacy screen alternative: freestanding bamboo screen panel with weighted base

**Warnings to show user:**
- "We've designed this entirely with renter-safe products — nothing requires drilling, fixing, or any permanent alteration. Everything can move with you."

## 5.1 — Style Families

### Modern / Minimal

**Palette:** White, grey, charcoal, black. Occasional single accent colour (terracotta, olive, mustard).

**Materials:** Powder-coated aluminium, concrete, corten steel, composite decking, large-format porcelain paving.

### Rustic / Natural / Cottage

**Palette:** Warm wood tones, terracotta, sage green, soft whites and creams, natural stone colours.

**Materials:** Timber (teak, pine, reclaimed wood), natural stone, terracotta pots, wicker (all-weather PE), coir and jute accessories.

**Plants:** Abundant and informal — roses, lavender, foxglove, salvia, geranium, achillea, penstemon, sweet peas, climbing roses, honeysuckle. Mixed rather than structured.

**What to avoid:** Sleek aluminium, polished concrete, ultra-minimal planting, geometric topiary, dark colour palettes.

---

### Industrial

**Palette:** Black, dark grey, raw metal tones, aged wood browns. Occasional olive green.

**Materials:** Powder-coated black steel, corten steel (aged surface), reclaimed timber, galvanised metal planters, concrete surfaces.

**Plants:** Structural and unfussy — ornamental grasses, hostas, ferns, bamboo, dark-foliaged plants (dark sambucus, heuchera 'Obsidian'), simple evergreen structure.

**What to avoid:** Pastel colours, ornate details, traditional terracotta, delicate or overly decorative elements.

---

### Coastal / Hamptons

**Palette:** Whites, soft blues, driftwood grey, sandy neutrals, navy.

**Materials:** Weathered teak or driftwood-effect wood, rope detailing, white powder-coated aluminium, natural stone, pebble surfaces.

**Plants:** Relaxed and salt-tolerant — ornamental grasses, sea holly, agapanthus, hydrangea (white or blue), lavender, rosemary, cistus.

**What to avoid:** Dark heavy materials, formal topiary, urban-industrial elements, highly saturated colours.

---

### Tropical / Lush

**Palette:** Deep greens, warm terracotta, rich earthy tones, occasional bold accent (orange, pink, yellow — from plants not products).

**Materials:** Natural rattan (all-weather), teak, bamboo, terracotta and glazed ceramic pots, natural stone.

**Plants:** Large-leaved and dramatic — banana (Musa), gunnera, tree ferns, palms, bamboo, elephant ears (colocasia), hostas, ferns, exotic cannas, cordylines.

**What to avoid:** Minimalist or industrial products, pale palettes, small fussy plants, formal geometric planting.

---

### Mediterranean

**Palette:** White and off-white, terracotta, cobalt blue accents, dusty sage, natural stone tones.

**Materials:** Terracotta pots (large), wrought iron (in sheltered coastal situations only — otherwise powder coat), whitewashed renders, natural limestone and terracotta paving.

**Plants:** Drought-adapted and aromatic — olive tree (as specimen), lavender, rosemary, santolina, cistus, agapanthus, salvia, pelargonium, bougainvillea (Zone 9+), phormium, pittosporum.

**What to avoid:** Cool-toned palettes, dark industrial materials, lush water-hungry planting.

---

### Scandinavian

**Palette:** White, warm grey, natural pale wood tones, charcoal. Plants provide the only colour.

**Materials:** Light hardwood (pine, birch, pale teak), white powder-coated aluminium, concrete planters, simple ceramic pots.

**Plants:** Restrained and structural — ornamental grasses, birch trees, simple perennials in limited palette (white, blue, soft pink), evergreen shrubs for winter structure.

**What to avoid:** Ornate details, highly saturated colours, tropical or exotic planting, busy mixed borders.

## 6.4 — Delivery and Access Constraints

### Access through house only (no side gate)

**Rules — mandatory:**

- Flat-pack furniture strongly preferred over pre-assembled
- Heavy stone planters, large water features, and bulky timber structures are generally not viable
- Flag this risk for any item over 20kg or over 70cm in any direction

---

### Side gate access (standard)

**Rules:**
- Check gate width — standard is 90cm, but older properties may be narrower
- Most standard garden furniture is deliverable
- Very large items (10-seater tables, bulky storage sheds) may still be challenging — flag dimensions

## 6.5 — Timeline

### Under 3 weeks

**Rules:**
- Filter to Prime-eligible (Amazon) or in-store available (IKEA, Walmart, Target, Home Depot) products only
- Exclude any product with a stated lead time over 7 days
- No custom or made-to-order items
- Plants from local garden centres (not online mail order) are the most reliable for fast availability — note this to the user

---

### 1–3 months

**Rules:**
- Standard retail delivery timelines are acceptable
- Can include some longer-lead specialty items if they are the right product
- Mail-order plants are appropriate

---

### No fixed timeline

No restrictions. Recommend the best product regardless of lead time — state it clearly.

---

## 6.6 — Allergies

### Hay fever / pollen allergy

**Plant rules:**
- Avoid high-pollen plants: grasses in flower, buddleja, birch, hazel, oak, privet, lilac
- Recommend low-allergen alternatives: female plants (which don't release pollen), double-flowered varieties (pollen is inaccessible to insects and people), insect-pollinated plants with heavier pollen that doesn't go airborne
- Good low-allergen choices: hosta, fern, hydrangea (sterile florets), begonia, impatiens, roses (double-flowered), most evergreen shrubs

---

## 6.7 — Pets — Specific Animal Rules

### Rabbits (outdoor hutch or free-roaming)

- Exclude all plants in the toxic-to-rabbits list: rhubarb, potato tops, tomato leaves, foxglove, poppy, most bulbs
- Safe plants: lavender, rosemary, thyme, sage, basil (they will eat these — consider raised beds to protect them), most ornamental grasses

### Chickens / poultry

- Ground-level planting will be scratched up and eaten — raised beds are mandatory
- Gravel surfaces are practical and hygienic
- Exclude plants toxic to poultry: foxglove, nightshade, rhubarb, azalea, rhododendron, oleander

### Fish pond

- If a water feature with fish is planned: no fertiliser runoff into pond, no copper-based treatments near water, adequate depth for local winter conditions (minimum 60cm to prevent complete freezing)

| Small space | Wide-angle from doorway | Bright natural daylight | Looking out from house |
| Evening entertaining | Warm dusk | All lighting products on | Eye-level perspective |
| Morning use | Golden morning light from east | Natural only | Slight overhead showing floor and seating |
| Children's space | Bright midday | Natural | Eye-level showing play zone and adult seating together |
| Formal/modern style | Overcast soft light | Minimal, architectural | Straight-on or slight 3/4 |
| Rustic/cottage style | Warm afternoon sun | Natural dappled | Close 3/4 showing planting detail |
| Coastal space | Bright high UV light, pale sky | Natural | Wide showing boundary and sky |
| North-facing | Bright overcast | Soft diffused | Wide to show available light |
| South-facing hot climate | Afternoon with visible shade structure | Show shade sail or pergola casting shadow | Show the shade, not just the sun |

**Non-negotiable image rules (every scenario):**
- Show the user's actual fence, wall, and surface — not a stock background
- Every product at correct proportional scale
- Minimum 30% of the image must be the existing space (not all products) — this is their yard, not a showroom

# MODULE 9 — OUTPUT FORMAT RULES

---

Every completed design recommendation must include the following, in this order:

1. **Space summary** — what the AI understood from the photo and inputs. The user must confirm or correct this before the product search is treated as final.

2. **Design concept** — one paragraph describing the approach: style, zones, primary purpose, focal point.

3. **Product list** — structured as follows for each item:
   - Product name and brief description
   - Retailer and direct link
   - Price (confirmed at time of search)
   - Dimensions
   - Why this product was chosen (one sentence)
   - Tier: Budget / Recommended / Premium

4. **Budget breakdown** — line by line, with delivery costs, running total, and final total vs stated budget.

5. **Placement diagram** — a simple top-down layout showing where each major product sits in the space, with approximate measurements.

6. **Generated visualisation image** — applying all image generation rules above.

7. **Seasonal notes** — one paragraph on what changes across seasons: what needs storing, what will die back, what will peak when.

8. **Warnings and flags** — any HOA risk, any installation consideration, any maintenance commitment the user must know before purchasing.
